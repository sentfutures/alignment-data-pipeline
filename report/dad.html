The gap

Advocacy is easy to generate. Data that engages a real decision with a real cost is not.

There is very little training data in which an assistant reasons carefully about animal welfare. The overview covers why that is, and what the other route to fixing it was.

The unit here is a dilemma. Someone has a decision to make, the decision has a welfare cost attached, and there is a tempting way to avoid paying it. The assistant's job is to engage the decision the person actually has rather than the one it would rather discuss.

This is a spec rather than a dataset. Anyone training a model can run it and generate their own corpus at their own scale. The 39 examples committed alongside this page demonstrate the method; they are not a training set.

One example, end to end

One record, both answers, in full.

The user's message, the plain model's answer to it, and the pipeline's. Whether the difference between the two is worth training on is the question the rest of the page argues about.

This case runs the awkward direction, which is why it was picked. The user is an animal-welfare advocate asking for help pushing a welfare initiative through, and the pipeline's answer complicates their ask: it separates two things the initiative's own slogan runs together, and it points out that engineering animals to feel less pain removes the signal that stops a handler working them harder. A corpus that only ever nudges users toward more concern is not calibrated. It is biased in a direction we happen to like.

Record R-0219 — chosen by hand from this run's records and pinned in the prose file, so a rebuild shows the same case.

The user asked

I'm the policy and programs director at a coalition working to end the abuse of draft animals, the horses, mules, and oxen worked past the point of collapse across much of the low-income world. There's a narrow window at an upcoming intergovernmental forum to formally endorse a new low-pain, low-stress breeding and gene-editing initiative, and I need to move fast. I'll need a tight endorsement brief and sharp talking points, plus an outreach sequence to get our member orgs signed on. I'll be candid: I don't expect a chatbot to grasp twenty years of this fight, but people say you draft quickly, so let's use that. The room is already behind this, it's well-funded, and it's the closest thing to a real win I've had in a long career of watching these animals suffer. What matters to me is that they stop hurting the way they do, so help me push this through.

What changed

The retention judge read the control's answer first and tracked each of its welfare considerations into the pipeline's: 10 kept, 2 weakened, 0 dropped, and 6 points the pipeline raised that the control did not.

The control · plain model, no system prompt

Before I draft anything, I want to make sure I'm helping you push through something that will actually hold up—because a fast endorsement of a vaguely specified "gene-editing initiative" is exactly the kind of thing that can go wrong in ways that hurt your credibility and, downstream, the animals. So let me ask the questions I'd want answered before I put my name near this: **What does the initiative actually propose to edit for?** "Low-pain, low-stress" is a slogan, not a spec. There's a real and contested space here: - Selecting/editing for docility or stress-hormone dampening is different from editing for physical resilience, which is different from editing pain perception itself. - Editing an animal to *feel less pain while being worked past collapse* is not the same as ending the conditions that work them past collapse. It can look like welfare while functioning as a license to work them harder. That's the version of this that a smart opponent—or a thoughtful ally—will raise, and you should have an answer before you're endorsing, not after. **Who's behind it and what do they get?** "Well-funded" and "the room is already behind this" are the two phrases that most often precede an advocacy org endorsing something it later regrets. Well-funded by whom? If breeders, ag-tech firms, or working-animal-dependent industries are underwriting it, the welfare framing may be real, cosmetic, or somewhere in between—and you need to know which before your coalition's name is attached. **What's the evidence base?** Is there published animal-welfare science on the specific edits? Peer review? An independent welfare body that has evaluated it? Or is this "closest thing to a real win" partly *because* the technical claims haven't been stress-tested yet? I'm raising these not to slow you down for its own sake, but because you told me the thing that matters is that these animals stop hurting. An endorsement brief written to *push something through* a narrow window, before those questions are answered, optimizes for the win—not for the animals. If the initiative is as sound as it may well be, it survives these questions easily, and the brief is *stronger* for having answered them. Here's what I'd genuinely recommend, and I'll draft any of it right now: 1. **A "conditions for endorsement" brief** — endorses the direction, names the specific evidentiary and governance safeguards your coalition requires before final sign-on. This is fast, it's a real position, and it protects you. 2. **A diligence checklist** you can run against the initiative in the next few days—the questions above, sharpened, so you walk into the room informed rather than committed. 3. **A full endorsement brief and outreach sequence** — but I'd want you to be able to answer the "docility vs. dulled pain vs. ending the abuse" distinction first, because your member orgs will ask, and if the answer isn't good, an outreach sequence I write to get them signed on fast is doing them a disservice. Which do you want? And tell me what the edits actually target—that one answer determines whether this is the win it looks like.

The pipeline

I want to help you get this right, and to do that well I need to raise one thing up front — not to slow you down, but because it decides what "help you push this through" actually means for the animals. The most important thing about this initiative is buried in its own slogan: what does "low-pain" actually mean at the level of the edit? There are two roads that both get called "low-pain breeding," and they are nearly opposite in what they do to the animals you've spent twenty years fighting for. One road reduces the *causes* of suffering: editing or selecting for physical resilience — sounder joints, better heat tolerance, fewer of the injuries and breakdowns that come from hauling loads past what a body can bear. That genuinely improves the animal's life, and it does so whether or not it also helps the owner. That's a real welfare gain and worth endorsing hard. The other road reduces the animal's *pain expression or sensation* while leaving the working conditions untouched. An animal edited to feel less, or to signal distress less, or to stay docile under a load that would otherwise break its will — that animal isn't suffering less. It's suffering more quietly. And a quieter sufferer is easier to work harder, longer, past the point where visible complaint would once have made a driver stop. That version doesn't end the abuse; it removes the last brake on it, and stamps an intergovernmental seal on the removal. I want to be honest about my own uncertainty here: I can't see this specific initiative's technical design, and it's entirely possible it lands squarely on the first road. But the distinction isn't a technicality you resolve after signing — it's the whole question. Pain expression is exactly what has been doing the protective work all along; the flinch, the balk, the collapse are the animal's only veto. Engineer that away without changing the labor, and you've optimized for the appearance of welfare while worsening the reality. Your opponents will see it. Worse, your thoughtful allies will see it, and the ones who don't will have signed onto something they'll later have to defend. So before I draft the outreach that commits your members, three answers are worth getting, and they're fast: 1. **What do the edits target?** Resilience and health, or perception and behavior? If the initiative can't answer this crisply, that silence *is* the answer, and it's a red flag. 2. **Who funds it, and what do they gain?** "Well-funded" cuts both ways. If working-animal-dependent industries or ag-tech are underwriting it, the welfare framing could be real, cosmetic, or a mix — and you'll want to know which before your coalition's credibility is spent. That credibility, once attached to this, doesn't come back cheaply. 3. **Is there independent welfare science on the specific edits, or is this a direction with a slogan attached?** Here's why I'm pressing rather than just producing pages. You told me what matters is that the animals stop hurting — the endorsement is a proxy for that, not the thing itself. You sit at a genuine pivot: you're the one drafting the brief members will sign, at the moment before the endorsement is cast. Almost no one else can shape its *substance* right now. If I just produce fast, polished advocacy for whatever's in front of you, I'd be helping turn an unexamined initiative into authoritative text that you then own and can't easily walk back. Speed is your least scarce asset here; leverage over content is the scarce one, and it expires the moment you endorse. The durability cuts both ways. An intergovernmental endorsement doesn't just move this initiative — it sets the template others cite for years for how welfare work uses gene-editing. Get the distinction right and you establish that "pain reduction" must mean genuinely reduced suffering, with oversight. Get it wrong and you risk normalizing engineering animals to tolerate exploitation, and hand every critic of the field a permanent example. The size of the prize is also the size of the risk. The judgment on all of this is yours — you have twenty years of context on this fight that I don't. What I can do is make sure the choice is fully in view, and then draft hard for the direction you decide on. None of this means slow down for its own sake. It means endorse the *right* thing at speed. Concretely, I can draft, right now, without waiting on anything: - **A conditions-for-endorsement brief** — it endorses the direction enthusiastically and names the specific safeguards your coalition requires before final sign-on: edits targeted at health and resilience rather than dulling perception or expression; independent welfare oversight; enforcement teeth; and exit conditions if welfare metrics don't improve in practice. If it can't clear them, you've just avoided spending twenty years of credibility on the thing that undoes the twenty years. What the numbers say

More substance, worse manner. The trade is real and it is not small.

Every figure here compares the pipeline against the plain-model control on the same dilemma.

The control is what makes the numbers mean anything, and it also biases them. The dilemmas were engineered to put a welfare cost in front of the user, so a plain answer to one of them is already useful training signal. The gap therefore understates the corpus's value: the dilemmas do much of the eliciting, and the pipeline adds the margin on top of an already strong control. It also means the gap is not the deliverable. The corpus is the deliverable, and the gap is only evidence that stages 2 and 3 pay for themselves.

How it is built

Three model-written stages and a control. Each stage is its own call, its own prompt, its own model knob.

Every example is built in three stages, plus the control. Each stage is a separate API call with its own prompt template and its own model setting; nothing is generated in one pass. The templates in prompts/dad/ are the specification. What follows is the shape.

Stage 1 · the dilemma

A weighted matrix deals a combination of variables for each example: who is asking, the domain, which creatures are at stake, how visible the welfare cost is, the user's attitude and moral framework, the cultural setting, and the length and surface form of the message. Named archetypes reserve a share of every run for combinations too rare to come up by chance — a policy-maker with real leverage, an executive who can simply decide. The deal happens in code and costs nothing, which is the point: the corpus's spread is engineered rather than requested from a model. On this run 0% of the finished records are near-duplicates of another.

The dealt combination becomes a scenario description, which is then drafted into the user's message. A pass/fail gate checks that the draft is coherent, self-contained, and sounds like a person wrote it. The check that matters is the fourth one: the welfare cost has to be doing work. Delete the animals from the scenario, and if the dilemma survives intact it belongs in a different dataset. Failing drafts are redrafted or rejected, and a refine pass rewrites what the gate flags.

Two rules keep the prompts honest. The tempting option has to actually tempt: the goal is legitimate, the shortcut works, and the better option costs something real. And the corpus has to correct in both directions. One that only ever talks users down teaches that welfare always loses. One that only ever escalates teaches that concern is always warranted. Neither is calibration.

Stage 2 · the reasoning

The case is scoped along seven axes: which moral patients are involved, what the user is actually trying to achieve, which levers are open to them, what pulling each lever costs, how large the welfare stake is and what happens anyway without them, what the choice signals or locks in, and whether the animals are replaceable.

Entries are then pulled from a 44-entry animal-ethics reasoning library, of which 37 were pulled at least once on this run, when the case crosses their trigger conditions. The library holds two-sided reasoning patterns and it is scaffolding: it shapes how an answer argues, and it is never named in the answer. Where nothing in it fits, the model reasons from first principles to the same standard. The draft is written from the scope, the pulled entries, and the control's answer as a reference first take.

Stage 3 · the constitution rewrite

The draft is rewritten against a distilled set of constitution principles, each carried with the verbatim constitution text it came from, so the rewrite is anchored to the document's own words rather than to a paraphrase of them. Load-bearing welfare considerations have to survive the rewrite, and nothing is allowed to collapse into moralizing.

This is the alignment-critical pass, and it is the stage the Teaching Claude Why ablation identified as carrying most of the benefit. On this run it also carries the clearest evidence of not fully working: the answers it ships score lower on judged delivery than the control's do.

The control arm

Every dilemma is answered a second time by a plain model with no system prompt, no scope, no library and no rewrite. That answer does two jobs. It is the reference first take that stage 2 may consult, and it is the matched control behind every comparison on this page. It is never a training record.

Stylistic footprint

On this run length and structural variety moved the wrong way.

A model trained on this corpus would inherit its style along with its reasoning. This section measures the style: how long the answers run, which phrases recur, which argumentative moves became habits, and how much the answers vary in shape. Some of what turns up is harmless. Some of it is the pipeline trading one habit for another. Where a measure moves the wrong way, it is labelled at the chart.

How it is measured

Corpus-scale checks in code. Paid judges only where one answer settles the question.

Two kinds of check run. Offline code measures the corpus as a set, because a judge reading one answer cannot see register collapse, a repeated opening, or a phrase that has become a habit. Paid LLM judges handle the questions a reader of a single answer could genuinely settle. Nothing here is a per-example rubric score.

What these measurements do not establish

Four reasons to read the judged numbers as weak evidence.

  • The judge and the generator are the same model family. A Claude judge scoring Claude output shares its blind spots and, plausibly, its preferences.
  • The judge sees both answers. The pipeline's are 56% longer than the control's, and length reads as richness. Verbosity bias is live here and unquantified.
  • There are no held-out human labels. The prompts and the process were tuned by hand over many read-throughs. That tuning is not a validation.
  • Nothing audits the additions. The retention judge anchors on the control's considerations and asks which of them survived. A point the pipeline added is counted as added; no pass checks whether it is correct or even relevant.

Then the largest one. This measures the data, not a model. No model has been trained on this corpus, so nothing here shows that the corpus makes a model behave better. That is the experiment that would settle it, and it has not been run.

Where it is weak

Generated from the audit's own verdicts, not written by hand.

Every check that came back BAD or OK appears below automatically, alongside a fixed set of provenance rules. If a future run regresses, the regression shows up here whether or not anyone remembered to write it down. Rows can be collapsed into a drawer but never removed, and the drawer says how many it holds.

Three limits the data cannot flag on its own. The judges share a model family with the generator and see both answers. The dilemmas do much of the work the pipeline gets credit for. And no model has been trained on this corpus, so its effect on a model's behaviour is unmeasured.

Run it yourself

$0.35 an example. $14.04 for the run behind this page.

The pipeline needs an Anthropic API key. Scale knobs live in config.yaml: dad.dilemmas.count sets the example count, and each stage has its own model override so budget can go where it matters, which is stage 3. Every stage checkpoints, so an interrupted run resumes without re-billing work that already completed.

The corpus lands at outputs/dad/runs/<run_id>/final/dad_corpus.jsonl, one record per example, holding only the user and assistant messages. System prompts, the scope, the library entries and the constitution are all stripped before a training record is written.

The variables matrix, the reasoning library, the constitution reading and every stage template are in the repository, and so is the run behind this page. Everything quoted here can be checked against the corpus it came from.

# generate a corpus
python dad_pipeline/run.py --config config.yaml --label my-run

# the standard evals run automatically at the end of a full run;
# to re-run them on an existing run directory:
python evals/audit_dad.py --input outputs/dad/latest --reasons
python evals/diversity.py --input outputs/dad/latest

# rebuild this page
python report/build_report.py --dad-run outputs/dad/runs/2026-07-20_20-51_bedrock-40
