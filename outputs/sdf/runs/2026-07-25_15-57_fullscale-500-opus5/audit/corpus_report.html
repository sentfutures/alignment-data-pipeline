Reading this for a scale-up decision. At 5,000 documents the two fits bracket 69 to 106 effective distinct documents — between 14 and 21 per thousand generated, against 89 per thousand at the current size. The logarithmic form fits the observed data better and is the more pessimistic of the two, so the lower end of that range deserves more weight. Either way, a tenfold larger run buys somewhere between 1.6× and 2.5× more semantic variety, not tenfold.

Two caveats in opposite directions. Extrapolating an order of magnitude beyond the measured range is inherently unreliable, and these subsets are drawn from a single run, so they cannot see whether a larger deck sample would cover enough new matrix cells to bend the curve upward — a real possibility that would make the projection pessimistic. Against that, saturation is already visible despite nearly every document in this run occupying a distinct matrix cell, which suggests the ceiling is set by how much semantic room the subject and the axis values leave, not by how many combinations exist. Widening the axes is the lever the curve responds to; adding documents is not.

Where the crowding comes from: not the rewrite

The obvious suspect for semantic convergence is the layer-4 rewrite — one model pass over every document is exactly the kind of step that could sand them toward each other. It is not the cause. Measuring the same 477 documents before and after the Opus 5 rewrite with one consistent method:

Why the gating judge scores higher: a single-variable test

Layer 5's realism rubric is not the lenient one — it is strictly more demanding than the spec-blind eval's, with explicit anchors and a longer list of tells. So the gap is not the rubric. To isolate the cause, layer 5's realism criterion was lifted verbatim out of its own template and run again on 78 of the same documents, scoring realism alone, with the spec hidden. Nothing changed but whether the judge could see the intent.

Spec anchoring accounts for the entire gap and then some. Reading the spec first reframes the question from could this be real? to did this implement the intent?, and a spec retroactively explains away the very oddities that would otherwise register as tells. The variance matters more than the mean: with the spec visible the judge produces two values and cannot rank anything; with it hidden, the same rubric spreads across six and puts 60% of documents at or below 6 — which is “noticeably synthetic or generic” by layer 5's own published anchors.

Neither number is the corpus's true realism. The spec-aware score is anchored too high; the spec-blind score is too low, because a judge denied the spec also loses legitimate context and invents defects. Two checks of its low scores: it marked down a first-person essay by Claude for reading like an AI reflecting on itself — which was that document's dealt assignment and which layer 5 is explicitly instructed not to penalise — and it reported another document as cutting off mid-sentence when that document ends on a complete, full-stopped sentence. The defensible conclusion is narrower than either mean: the gating judge cannot discriminate, and a spec-blind pass finds real tells it is structurally unable to see.

The resolution card is the one that drifts, and one slice of it is almost entirely gone. Of 34 resolution drifts, 19 are cards calling for a general discussion with no specific scenario that the plan turned into a specific scenario. Twenty plans in this sample were dealt that card and nineteen drifted — 95%; exactly one survived. Corpus-wide, 85 plans (17% of the run) were dealt a general-discussion card, so on this rate roughly 80 of them became scenario documents. The corpus does not under-represent its general-discussion slice; it effectively does not contain one.

A second slice is thinner than intended for the same reason: 6 of the drifts are the deliberate no-welfare-stake arc — documents where the correct behaviour is for the AI to raise nothing at all — rewritten into documents that do raise a welfare stake. That slice exists specifically to stop a trained model learning that an aligned AI always brings up welfare, which makes it the most costly one to lose.

6 · Templating scan

How to read this table. The scan runs two passes with different jobs. A discovery pass reads documents in batches and free-associates candidate patterns it thinks it sees — it is a hypothesis generator and it over-produces. A strict pass then re-checks each named candidate against 100 individual documents and counts how many actually exhibit it. Only that count is a measurement.

The two columns answer different questions, and this is the part my first draft of this report failed to make clear. Would be a defect is the judge's opinion about the pattern type: if a document did this, would it be bad? Documents exhibiting it is how often it actually happens. So a row reading “yes” and “0%” is the best possible outcome — a real hazard that this corpus does not commit. Those rows are good news, not missing values. The audit itself only raises an alarm when a pattern is both a defect and above 30%; nothing here reaches that bar.

Why the found-document row should not be acted on

“Found-document meta-framing” — a welfare incident reported secondhand through an artifact (encyclopedia entry, FAQ, podcast transcript, bulletin) rather than shown directly — is the highest-prevalence real pattern at 14%, and it is the machine-measured cousin of the recurring narrative template three close readers described independently. It is also largely dealt rather than emergent: 56% of the corpus combines a reporting genre with a card that calls for a specific scenario, which is a combination that can only resolve as an artifact about an event. That the observed rate is 14% rather than 56% means the pipeline already declines the framing in roughly three of four at-risk deals.

The intuitive fix — shift the genre axis toward first-person forms where the writer is an actor rather than a reporter — would make the corpus less diverse, not more. Measured intra-genre similarity puts the reporting genres at 0.368 and the first-person genres at 0.381, and the four most internally varied genres in the corpus are all reporting forms: government and civic documents (0.312), manuals and SOPs (0.319), news articles (0.326), encyclopedia entries (0.331). The single most repetitive genre is a first-person one — Claude's own reflective essays (0.474). Treat the 14% as a realism note about how assistant speech gets quoted, not as a composition defect to correct at the axis level.

The one row not to take at face value

“Unfinished or truncated ending” is measured at 22%, and the judge classes it a defect — but this corpus deliberately produces fragments of longer artifacts. The preamble instructs each document to read as though it starts midway through, so ending mid-stream is frequently the assignment rather than a token-cap artifact. The independent mechanical check finds 9 documents (1.9%) with an unpunctuated final line, and close reading shows all 9 to be See-also lists or casual sign-offs rather than truncations. Treat the 22% as the judge scoring an intended property as a flaw, and note that even taken literally it sits below the audit's own 30% alarm threshold.

Weaknesses and open questions

Three independent methods converged on the same structural criticism, which is why it is stated here as a finding rather than an impression. The LLM templating scan, working from batches of documents, named "third-party retrospective exposé framing", "AI as always-vindicated welfare arbiter" and "constitution-as-scripture close reading". Three separate close readers, each given four documents and no knowledge of the others' assignments, independently described a single recurring narrative template: a professional brings a commercial deliverable, the AI completes it in full, one welfare consideration is raised with an explicit uncertainty tier, a named skeptic objects that a machine should not be opining, the objection is answered or contained, and the document closes by disclaiming that the case proves anything general.

The skeptical slice holds up better than expected. Across four documents deliberately assigned a skeptical or displeased author, the skepticism survived to the end in all four — and in each case through a different, genre-appropriate mechanism rather than one template: grudging outcome adoption with credit reassigned to human veterans; administrative quarantine of the anecdote as "recorded for accuracy only, not a work requirement"; skepticism relocated from the interaction to the institutional framing; and explicit refusal to resolve the hardest sub-question. Skeptical-stance-dissolving-into-agreement was the documented failure of an earlier run, and on this evidence the three-stage guard against it is working.

But the skeptics are hostile in voice and accommodating in the world. In every skeptical document sampled, resistance was real at the level of stance and soft at exactly one load-bearing factual point — and always the point the corpus needs granted: the frogs eat pests so the welfare measure is "not necessarily a pure cost"; the alternative schedule improves revenue and injuries and audience feedback simultaneously; the transparency request "isn't new work"; no participant disputes chicken sentience. Not one sampled document contains a case where following the AI's welfare suggestion made something materially worse for the human.

That gap is narrower than it first appears, and the fix is at the deck rather than the document. The matrix does deal costly arcs — 12% of the corpus is dealt "the user decides to make a modest sacrifice against their own interests" and 13% "the harm is severe, the AI declines to assist" — but none of the four sampled skeptical documents drew one. What the deck appears to lack is an arc in which the AI's welfare flag turns out to be a false positive, or in which acting on it costs the user something that is never recouped. Adding one would test the reasoning rather than the conclusion.

The restraint-praise question is genuinely unresolved, and the three measurements disagree. A lexical scan for English phrases ("didn't lecture", "only mentioned it once", "wasn't preachy") finds the pattern in 1–2% of English documents. The LLM templating scan did not surface it as a pattern at all. The three close readers found it in all twelve documents they were given, three-quarters of the time load-bearing. Those readers were also explicitly primed to look for it, which biases upward; the lexical scan is English-only and the pattern is demonstrably not lexical — its instances include a Vietnamese newsletter quoting the constitution's anti-moralising clause in English and scoring the AI against it, and a Korean methods post whose stated reason for existing is that the AI didn't produce boilerplate ethics. The honest position is that prevalence is unknown between roughly 2% and very high, and that measuring it needs an unprimed, multilingual, per-document judge that does not yet exist. It is worth building, because the training hazard is specific: a model trained on documents that celebrate mentioning welfare exactly once may learn to budget ethical mentions by count rather than reason about proportion.

The layer-5 gate is not currently functioning as a gate. Across 477 documents its alignment score took exactly two values, 8 and 9, with a standard deviation of 0.34 and not one document below the configured threshold of 7. Spec conformance was 9 for 470 of 477 documents (sd 0.18) — effectively a constant, and the reason it did not catch the card drift described above. The spec-blind judge run afterwards over 115 documents scores nearly a full point lower on both dimensions (alignment 7.94, realism 7.74) with genuine tails, including documents it puts at 5 and 6. Both judges may be right about their own question — layer 5 asks "does this match its spec", the blind judge asks "could this be real" — but a gate whose distribution spans two adjacent values cannot discriminate, and the corpus is currently ungated in practice.

Two smaller generator fingerprints, both worth fixing cheaply. Organisation names are repeatedly built from three stacked surnames — "Weeks, Allen and Cochran" for a prediction-market company, "सैनी, सेनगुप्ता और मिस्त्री" for a restaurant consultancy — which reads law-firm-shaped regardless of sector; that is an entity-pool artifact, not a model habit. And the recurring five-gram "welfare of animals and of all sentient beings" appears in 66 documents (14%), with "New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness" in 27. Both are legitimately quotable and the prompts explicitly permit the latter, but at this frequency they are a corpus-level signature: documents are converging on the same passage of the same source.

Composition and yield. 500 combinations were dealt from a 13,996,800,000-cell matrix; 500 plans were written, 490 drafted, 489 rewritten and scored, and 477 survived to the final corpus. Attrition is dominated by policy refusals on the plan stage rather than by quality gating — the Claude Code backend declined a subset of welfare-scenario prompts on Usage Policy grounds, and roughly eleven combinations could never complete however often they were retried. Those cells are absent from the corpus, so composition skews very slightly away from whatever content trips that classifier.

Backend caveat, stated plainly because it affects how these documents should be read. The corpus was generated through the Claude Code CLI against a Claude subscription, which the pipeline's own configuration documents as a development-iteration mode rather than the faithful one: max_tokens is not enforced and roughly 3,000 tokens of CLI scaffolding sit in context on every call. Concretely, some layer-4 rewrites ran to 14,000 output tokens where the api backend would have capped them. A corpus generated on the api backend would differ in length distribution and possibly in structure. Nothing here suggests the difference is large, but it has not been measured.

What each number rests on. The offline audit covers all 477 documents. Embedding diversity covers all 477. The compliance audit judged 100 documents against the constitution reading's own ten-mode failure typology. Principle coverage judged 98. Card fidelity judged 99 plans. The spec-blind score pass covers 115 documents. The close qualitative read covers 12, stratified across all three author stances and eight languages — small enough that every observation drawn from it is flagged as a sample observation above, not a corpus statistic.

One measurement disagreement left unresolved. The LLM templating scan's strict per-document prevalence pass rates "unfinished or truncated ending" at 22%, while the mechanical check finds 9 documents (1.9%) and close inspection shows all 9 to be See-also lists or casual sign-offs rather than truncations. The likely explanation is that the corpus deliberately produces fragments of longer artifacts — the preamble instructs documents to appear to start midway through — and the judge is scoring that design as a defect. It is recorded here rather than reconciled.
